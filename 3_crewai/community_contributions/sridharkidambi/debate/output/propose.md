The protest by the “Cockroach Janata Party” in Delhi against NEET and its related demands is **right** in principle, because it raises a legitimate concern: **a single, highly centralized exam can create severe pressure, unequal outcomes, and repeated allegations of unfairness**.

First, NEET has become more than just an entrance test; for many students, it is a high-stakes filter that decides their future in one shot. In a country as large and unequal as India, that is a serious problem. Students from elite coaching centers, urban schools, and better-funded families often get an advantage, while rural and economically weaker students face a much harder climb. A protest against this system is therefore a protest for **fairer opportunity**, not against merit.

Second, the repeated controversies around paper leaks, administrative failures, and uncertainty have damaged trust. When an exam is supposed to be the gateway to medical education, even one credible allegation of corruption is enough to justify public outrage. Protest is a democratic response when institutions fail to protect integrity.

Third, demanding reforms—such as more transparent testing, stronger anti-leak safeguards, and alternative admission routes—is reasonable. Even if one does not fully support a complete ban on NEET, the protest is still right because it pushes the system toward **accountability, equity, and student welfare**.

Real-world example: when any major public exam is compromised, the entire burden falls on students who have spent years preparing. In that context, silence would be acceptance. Protest is the voice of those who feel the system is not working for them.

So, in conclusion, the protest is **right** because it challenges inequality, demands fairness, and protects students from a flawed and overly centralized system.
